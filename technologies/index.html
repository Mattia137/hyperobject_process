Media Technologies

Spatial, Emissive & Acoustic Systems
01. Direct-View LED

Modular emissive panels composed of individual Light Emitting Diodes grouped into pixels. Unlike traditional LCDs which use a backlight, each LED produces its own light and color.

Originating in the late 20th century for stadium Jumbotrons and outdoor advertising, high-resolution direct-view LEDs surged in the 2010s, becoming the standard for virtual production volumes (like "The Mandalorian") due to their intense brightness and scalability.

02. Micro-LED

Displays utilizing ultra-fine, microscopic LEDs to form individual sub-pixels. They offer the infinite contrast of OLEDs without the risk of organic material degradation or burn-in.

Transitioning from R&D in the 2010s to ultra-premium consumer and commercial markets in the 2020s, Micro-LED represents the current apex of flat-panel display technology, combining extreme longevity with unparalleled brightness.

03. OLED Displays

Organic Light Emitting Diodes are self-emissive organic films that glow when an electrical current is applied. Because pixels can be completely turned off, they achieve true black levels and infinite contrast.

Following early commercialization in the 2000s, OLEDs dominated the 2010s smartphone and premium television markets. Their thin, flexible nature has also enabled rollable and bendable architectural display concepts.

04. Transparent Emissive

OLED or LED arrays manufactured on transparent substrates. When pixels are turned off, the panel functions like clear glass; when activated, digital imagery appears suspended in mid-air.

Long relegated to science fiction, transparent displays became commercially viable in the mid-2010s. They are increasingly used in retail, museums, and augmented architectural partitions where sightlines must be maintained.

05. Laser Projection

Single-channel, high-lumen projectors that replace traditional xenon or mercury lamps with solid-state laser light sources, routing light through DLP or LCD imaging chips.

Widely adopted in the 2010s, laser phosphor technology revolutionized projection by eliminating lamp replacements, vastly expanding color gamuts, and allowing projectors to be mounted at any angle without thermal failure.

06. Edge-Blended Systems

The practice of overlapping multiple projectors to create a single, continuous ultra-widescreen image. Software gradients feather the overlapping edges to hide the seams.

Originating in the late 1990s as a solution to resolution and brightness limits, edge-blending is the foundational technique for large-scale immersive environments and panoramic theatrical backdrops.

07. Projection Mapping

A technique that turns physical 3D objects—often complex building facades—into display surfaces. Specialized software warps and masks the projected image to perfectly align with the object's geometry.

Emerging as an underground VJ technique in the early 2000s, it exploded into mainstream architectural spectacle by the 2010s, fundamentally altering how we perceive static urban environments at night.

08. Immersive Environments

Continuous wall, floor, and sometimes ceiling projection fields that physically enclose the viewer, creating an illusion of spatial presence without headsets.

Rooted in the CAVE (Cave Automatic Virtual Environment) systems of the 1990s academic research labs, these spaces entered the cultural mainstream in the late 2010s via widespread "immersive art" exhibitions.

09. Dome-Based Systems

Projection onto hemispherical surfaces utilizing fisheye lenses or multi-projector blends to create a fully enveloping, ceiling-bound image field.

Stemming from mid-20th-century analog planetariums and OMNIMAX theaters, the transition to digital "fulldome" projection in the 2000s opened the format to experimental media art and abstract spatial narrative.

10. Light-Field Displays

Displays that emit multiple light rays per pixel in different directions. This creates a multi-view, parallax-correct volume of light, allowing users to move their heads and see around 3D objects without wearing glasses.

A highly complex optical challenge, light-field arrays transitioned from theoretical physics to functional prototypes in the late 2010s, pushing toward the "holographic" screens of science fiction.

11. Volumetric Displays

Systems that construct 3D imagery using voxels (volumetric pixels) within a physical space, often via rapidly spinning screens (swept-volume) or lasers intersecting in specialized gases.

Explored since the mid-20th century for radar and medical imaging, true volumetric displays remain largely experimental, limited by physical mechanics and immense data rendering requirements.

12. True Holographic

Displays based on wavefront reconstruction, utilizing laser interference patterns to recreate the exact physical light waves that would bounce off a real object.

Invented conceptually in the 1940s and practically with lasers in the 1960s, static holograms are common. However, real-time, dynamic digital holography remains one of the ultimate, unresolved frontiers of display technology.

13. Head-Mounted VR

Opaque stereoscopic headsets that completely block out the physical world, replacing it with a digitally rendered environment tracked to the user's head movements.

Following the "Sword of Damocles" in the 1960s and a failed commercial push in the 1990s, VR finally achieved critical consumer viability in the 2010s, reshaping gaming and architectural pre-visualization.

14. Optical AR Displays

See-through systems (like Microsoft HoloLens) utilizing transparent glass waveguides. Small projectors shoot light into the edge of the lens, which bounces into the user's eye, overlaying digital data onto real-world views.

Pioneered for enterprise and military applications in the 2010s, these displays represent the purest form of Augmented Reality, though field-of-view limits have slowed widespread consumer adoption.

15. Video Pass-Through MR

Closed-view headsets that use high-resolution exterior cameras to feed real-time video of the outside world to interior screens, digitally inserting 3D elements into the video feed.

Gaining massive traction in the 2020s (e.g., Apple Vision Pro, Meta Quest 3), pass-through MR bypasses the optical limits of transparent glass, offering highly controlled, high-contrast mixed realities.

16. Wearable Spatial Computing

Lightweight, glasses-form-factor devices providing standalone AR or MR capabilities without bulky tethered computing, effectively merging digital interfaces with daily spatial perception.

Currently the holy grail of tech hardware for the mid-to-late 2020s, the ambition is to eventually replace the smartphone by dissolving the 2D screen into the physical environment.

17. Spatial Audio

Acoustic processing that localizes sound sources in full 3D space, taking height, distance, and direction into account relative to the listener's head position.

Stemming from 1970s ambisonics and theatrical surround formats, real-time object-based audio (like Dolby Atmos) became the invisible half of the VR revolution in the 2010s, creating necessary acoustic realism for immersive media.
